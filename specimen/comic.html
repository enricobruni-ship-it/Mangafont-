Two registers, one face

The capitals and the lowercase are drawn to different specifications. Caps come from a sumi-brush cut: high contrast, dramatic tapers, large terminal flags. Lowercase comes from a kanji-slab cut: near-monoline, heavy, counters held open.

This is ordinary type practice taken further than usual — caps are normally drawn a shade lighter than lowercase so the two colours match. The two registers sit together here because their stems were already close: 216 against 202. Had they been far apart, the line would read as two fonts sharing it.

Stroke overshoot

Both registers let strokes run past their joins rather than stopping at them, by 10 units in the caps and 36 in the lowercase. A large part of why kanji read as kanji at a glance is that strokes cross and stick out; stopping every stroke politely at its junction is what makes a Latin face built on kanji logic look merely decorative.

Mangafont Comic covers Latin and katakana, not kanji. A character such as 東 falls back to whatever else is installed, as it does here and in the first panel overleaf.
